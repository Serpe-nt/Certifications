<Problem>
An alert fires for the checkout service. The on-call engineer opens its dashboard and finds 24 panels: CPU, memory, garbage collection, threads, queue sizes, and somewhere among them the request rate. Two minutes later they still cannot say whether customers are affected.
</Problem>

A dashboard that shows everything answers nothing quickly. A good one starts from the question its reader brings.

<Idea>
A RED dashboard answers one question about a service: are its users being served well? It shows three things side by side: how many requests arrive (rate), how many fail (errors) and how long they take (duration). Everything else goes lower down or on another dashboard.
</Idea>

## RED for services, USE for machines

Grafana's best practices name two methods for choosing what to show. RED looks at the requests a service handles: rate, errors and duration. It reports what users feel, so it often shows the symptom of a problem, and Grafana advises alerting on these symptoms. USE looks at a resource such as a CPU, a disk or a network card: utilization, saturation and errors. It points at causes. The four golden signals from Google's SRE book add saturation to RED's three.

<Compare caption="Two methods, two kinds of thing to watch">
  <Col title="RED" hot>Rate, errors, duration. For services, especially microservices. Shows what users feel.</Col>
  <Col title="USE">Utilization, saturation, errors. For machines and resources. Points at causes.</Col>
</Compare>

## A layout that reads at a glance

A dashboard should tell one story, from general to specific. The best practices show RED with rate and errors on the left and duration on the right, one row per service. Keep each graph simple, give it a unit and a title that says what it shows, and use colour with a meaning: thresholds make red mean bad. When services differ a lot in size, compare like with like, so a large service does not drown a small one.

Duration needs more than an average. A percentile such as p95 is the value that 95% of requests stay under, so p50, p95 and p99 together show both the typical request and the slow tail. The lab's span metrics are histograms, so `histogram_quantile()` from module m04 gives these percentiles.

<Layers caption="A RED dashboard from top to bottom">
  <Layer title="Variable">Service, so one dashboard serves every service.</Layer>
  <Layer title="Rate and errors">Requests per second and the error share, side by side.</Layer>
  <Layer title="Duration">p50, p95 and p99 latency, in seconds.</Layer>
  <Layer title="Details" hot>Rows for what helps the diagnosis, collapsed until needed.</Layer>
</Layers>

## Rows keep the rest out of the way

Rows and tabs group panels under a title. A collapsed row costs nothing until someone opens it, so diagnostic panels can wait there without crowding the first screen. Grafana's maturity model also counts the habits around a dashboard: a meaningful name, an owner people can find, no lasting copies, and refresh only as often as the data changes.

<Question id="q-m05-s08" answer="c">
A team builds a RED dashboard for its payment service. Which set of panels is RED?

- CPU usage, memory saturation and disk errors

  These are USE signals for a machine, not for the requests a service handles.

- Uptime, version and number of replicas

  These describe the deployment. None of them shows how requests are served.

- Requests per second, error share and latency percentiles

  Rate, errors and duration of requests make up RED.

- Log lines per second, warnings and log volume by pod

  These describe logging. RED is about the requests a service handles.
</Question>
