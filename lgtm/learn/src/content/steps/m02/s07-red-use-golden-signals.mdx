<Problem>
A new service goes live and the team asks you to build its dashboard. It exposes two hundred metrics. If you put all of them on screen, nobody will find the important line during an incident. You need a short, fixed list of what to watch for every service and every machine.
</Problem>

You do not have to invent that list. Three well-known ones exist, and they differ mainly in what they watch.

<Idea>
Three well-known checklists give that list. RED is for services: rate, errors, duration. USE is for resources such as CPU and disks: utilization, saturation, errors. The four golden signals from Google's SRE book are latency, traffic, errors and saturation.
</Idea>

## RED watches what users feel

RED is for services. Rate is requests per second. Errors is the number of failing requests. Duration is how long requests take, as a distribution. These are what a user of the service notices: whether it answers, whether it fails, and how slow it is. So a good RED dashboard stands in for the user's experience, and it is the right place for alerts.

## USE watches the machines

USE is for resources: CPU, memory, disks and network devices. Utilization is how busy a resource is, such as CPU use. Saturation is how much work waits, such as a queue or the load average. Errors counts error events. USE does not tell you what users feel. It points at causes: when RED shows that checkout is slow, USE can show that its node is out of CPU.

## The four golden signals join both

Google's SRE book names four golden signals: latency, traffic, errors and saturation. Its advice: if you can measure only four metrics of a user-facing system, measure these. They are RED plus saturation, so they cover what users feel and add the first warning that a resource is filling up.

## Errors and the slow tail need care

Two things go wrong often when these signals are measured. The first is errors. Keep the latency of failed requests apart from successful ones. A fast error can hide in an average and make latency look better. A slow error is even worse than a fast one.

The second is the mean. An average hides the few very slow requests. The SRE book gives an example: a service with an average latency of 100 ms at 1,000 requests per second can easily have 1% of its requests take 5 seconds. Measure a distribution, with buckets or percentiles such as p99, so the slow tail shows.

<Trap>
Do not alert on USE signals alone. High CPU on one node may not hurt any user. Alert on symptoms users feel, such as errors and latency, and use USE dashboards to find the cause.
</Trap>

<Remember>
RED for services, USE for resources, and the golden signals when you can watch only four things. Measure latency as a distribution, and keep failed requests apart.
</Remember>

<Question id="q-m02-s07" answer="c">
A team wants one dashboard per microservice that shows how users experience it. Which method fits best?

- USE, because it measures how busy each resource is

  USE is for resources such as CPU and disks. It shows causes, not what users feel.

- Only CPU and memory per pod

  These are resource signals. A service can have spare CPU and still fail its users.

- RED: request rate, errors and duration for each service

  RED is made for services and works as a proxy for user experience.

- The average latency of all services together

  An average across services hides which service is slow, and the mean hides the slow tail.
</Question>
