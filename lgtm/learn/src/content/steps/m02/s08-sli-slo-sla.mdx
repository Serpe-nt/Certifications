<Problem>
Developers want to ship new features every day. Operations want fewer changes, because changes break things. Without a shared number for "reliable enough", every release turns into an argument. Users, too, need to know what they can expect from the service.
</Problem>

The way out is to agree on one number, a target for it, and what happens when the target is missed.

<Idea>
An SLI measures how good the service is for users. An SLO is the target for that measure. An SLA is a contract that says what happens when the target is missed. The error budget is the failure the SLO still allows, and it decides when to slow down releases.
</Idea>

## SLI: a number about the user's experience

A service level indicator is a carefully defined number about the service, such as the share of requests that succeed, or request latency. A good SLI measures the service as users see it. CPU use is a poor SLI: users do not feel it. The share of checkout requests that succeed is a good one: users feel every failure.

## SLO is the target, SLA is the contract

A service level objective is a target for an SLI over a time window. Example: 99.9% of checkout requests succeed over four weeks.

A service level agreement is a contract with users that includes consequences, often money back, when an SLO is missed. To tell them apart, ask "what happens if we miss it?". If nothing happens, it is an SLO, not an SLA.

  <Layer title="SLI">The measure: the share of checkout requests that succeed.</Layer>
  <Layer title="SLO">The target: 99.9% of them succeed over four weeks.</Layer>
  <Layer title="SLA" hot>The contract: what users get when the target is missed.</Layer>

## The error budget turns the argument into a rule

The error budget is 100% minus the SLO. With a 99.9% SLO and 3 million requests in four weeks, 3,000 requests may fail. An outage that fails 1,500 requests uses half the budget.

<Figures caption="A 99.9% SLO over four weeks, 3 million requests" unit="Failed requests">
  <Stat label="Error budget" value="3,000" amount={3000} max={3000} />
  <Stat label="Used by one outage" value="1,500" amount={1500} max={3000} hot note="Half the budget is gone." />
</Figures>

The budget is what ends the argument from the start. While budget is left, the team can ship. An error budget policy says what the team does when the budget runs out, for example: stop releases except urgent fixes until the service is back within its SLO.

## Why the target is never 100%

A target of 100% is unrealistic and blocks change. Users would not see 100% anyway: the networks and devices between them and you fail too. So each extra nine costs more and helps users less. An SLO below 100% always leaves a budget, and the budget is what lets the team keep shipping.

<Trap>
People often say "SLA" when they mean SLO. An internal target with no penalty is an SLO. Keep the two apart in interviews and in the exam.
</Trap>

<Remember>
The SLI is the measure, the SLO the target, the SLA the contract with a penalty. The error budget is 100% minus the SLO, and when it is spent, releases slow down.
</Remember>

<Question id="q-m02-s08" answer="b">
A service has an availability SLO of 99.5% over 30 days and served 2,000,000 requests in that window. How many failed requests does the error budget allow?

- 1,000

  That would be a 99.95% SLO. The budget is 0.5% of the requests.

- 10,000

  The budget is 100% minus 99.5%, so 0.5% of 2,000,000 requests.

- 99,500

  That mixes up the SLO percentage with a number of requests.

- 0, because an SLO allows no failures

  An SLO below 100% always leaves an error budget.
</Question>
